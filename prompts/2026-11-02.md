### America is firing up retired nuclear reactors to keep its AI data centres running
Lead: yes
The scramble for electricity to power artificial intelligence has pushed the United States to an unusual step: bringing shut-down nuclear plants back to life. Operators are recommissioning three previously retired reactors expected to return about 2.25 gigawatts to the grid, with the output already spoken for by large technology companies building AI data centres. In parallel, the US Department of Energy is lending the utility Constellation around five billion dollars to uprate reactors at several existing plants, adding roughly a further gigawatt, most of it earmarked for AI operators. Separately, Constellation struck a long-term deal to supply hundreds of megawatts to a major cloud provider's AI campuses.

Why it matters: Compute is no longer the only bottleneck for AI; power is. The decisions being made now about reactors, grids and long-term energy contracts will shape where the next generation of AI is built, and who can afford to build it.

### Norway raises its national cyber-risk level over AI, warning about agents that act on their own
Norway's National Security Authority has lifted the country's national cyber-risk level to high, pointing specifically to large language models and agentic AI — systems that can take actions, not just answer questions. The authority warns that the same tools that help defenders are lowering the skill and effort attackers need, letting them probe networks, write malicious code and run intrusions faster and at larger scale. It is one of the clearest moves yet by a national security agency to treat autonomous AI as a standing threat rather than a future risk.

Why it matters: When a government formally raises its threat level because of AI, it signals that agentic systems have moved from research demonstrations to a live concern for critical infrastructure, business and ordinary users alike. European regulators and security agencies are increasingly aligned on this warning.

# USA

### Four of the biggest AI companies agree on one way for websites to say "don't train on me"
OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic have agreed to honour a single shared signal that lets website owners refuse to have their content used for AI training. Publishers would add a token — effectively a "do not use for AI training" instruction — to the robots.txt file that already governs web crawlers, and the four companies say they will respect it. The draft was written by an Anthropic engineer and is voluntary. Rollout and scope differ: Anthropic has begun honouring it, OpenAI says it will "in the coming months", and Microsoft and Google are targeting 2027, with Google's version also covering the data behind Gemini's answers.

Why it matters: For years publishers have had no simple, common way to say no to AI training. A shared standard, if the companies actually enforce it, could give writers and news organisations real leverage over how their work is used. The caveats matter too: it is voluntary, it covers only future training, and it leaves today's scraping untouched.

# CHINA

### DeepSeek open-sources its coding agent and offers free web search to developers
China's DeepSeek has released Harness, an open-source coding and work agent published under a permissive MIT licence, built around an architecture in which everything is a plug-in. Alongside it, the lab put out an open-weight model in its V4.1 line and opened its Search and Fetch tools so they can be called for free from Harness and from rival coding assistants, with the free search offer running through the start of November. The agent is free to use, even as the company raised prices for access to its most capable paid model.

Why it matters: Giving away a capable agent and free search undercuts the pricing of Western coding tools and widens the audience for Chinese AI among developers worldwide. It is also a reminder that open-weight releases from China keep setting the pace on cost, even as both Washington and Beijing tighten rules on what AI can be exported.

# ASIA

### Japan brings its AI playbook to Southeast Asia's small businesses
Japan is extending its national AI strategy outward, hosting a focus session with ASEAN countries on a joint initiative to help micro, small and medium-sized firms across the region adopt AI. Japanese companies including Preferred Networks presented work on practical, domain-specific "vertical" AI and on "physical" AI that builds on the country's manufacturing strengths. It sits on top of Japan's larger domestic push — a homegrown AI model and a goal of putting millions of AI-equipped robots to work across more than a dozen sectors by 2040 — backed by heavy multi-year government funding, driven in part by a shrinking workforce.

Why it matters: Japan is positioning itself as a "trusted partner" offering an alternative to the US and Chinese AI ecosystems, pairing exports of technology with governance and skills. For Southeast Asia's smaller economies, the question is whether these partnerships translate into real capability on the ground rather than pilots.

# INDIA

### Bihar approves an AI Policy 2026 to court AI firms, capability centres and startups
Bihar's state cabinet has cleared an Artificial Intelligence Policy 2026, joining a growing list of Indian states writing their own AI playbooks. The policy offers incentives to attract AI companies, global capability centres and startups to the state, and promises support for homegrown ventures through incubation, financial help, mentoring and better access to markets. It also sets out provisions for AI governance alongside the push for investment and jobs.

Why it matters: India's AI story is increasingly being written at the state level, not just in Delhi. States competing on incentives, talent and data-centre-friendly rules will shape where investment and jobs actually land — and whether regions outside the established tech hubs can get a share of the AI build-out.

### Tata Communications plans a 450-million-dollar bet on AI-ready connectivity in India
Tata Communications has outlined plans to invest about 450 million dollars in building connectivity infrastructure in India tuned for artificial intelligence — the high-capacity, low-latency networking that links data centres, enterprises and AI services. The commitment comes as demand for AI compute inside India drives a wave of spending on the plumbing underneath it, from subsea and terrestrial links to the networks that move data between AI facilities.

Why it matters: Running AI at national scale is not only about chips and data centres; it depends on the networks that connect them. Investment of this size signals that Indian operators expect sustained domestic demand for AI services, and it is the kind of infrastructure spending that quietly determines how fast and how affordably AI reaches businesses and users across the country.
